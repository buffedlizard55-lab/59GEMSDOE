3. Why that geometry wins under the official metric (DERIVED)

The official metric is DTI = TP_w / (TP_w + 0.2·FP_w + 0.8·FN_w) with a 300 m (3 px) triangular kernel (problem page 967; implementation src/gems_dti/metric.py, transcribed and unit-tested here). Because TP_w + FN_w = G exactly, DTI = TP_w / (0.2·S_w + 0.8·G) with S_w = TP_w + FP_w, and adding one non-redundant dot of kernel credit k raises the score iff

k > 0.2 · DTI (proof in docs/research/utility-derivation.md; algebra verified 500/500 synthetic)

At the 0.2778 level the bar is k > 0.0556, i.e. a dot pays only if it lands within ≈ 2.83 px (283 m) of a truth pixel. Three consequences decide the whole competition:

  1. Dot count is the primary risk. Every dot that misses the 283 m corridor costs the same as a

truthful dot earns. A sparse, high-precision set beats a dense, low-precision one at equal hit rate.

  1. Dots on already-mapped faults are free but worthless. DrivenData staff (2026-09-16) confirmed

that known USGS/INGENIOUS fault pixels are masked out of scoring entirely, so a dot there neither earns nor costs. The "prune" therefore removes only the flanks (unmasked cells 1–2 px off a known trace, which can never be truth and always cost).

  1. Coverage lattices need the tiled area to contain most of the truth. If a block lattice covers a

fraction a of the truth and the dots are placed on a 4 px lattice, TP ≈ 0.44·a·G; with G ≈ 30,000, n = 35,000 that is DTI ≈ 0.05 unless a is very large. This is exactly why the family's block-tiled designs stall around 0.25–0.28 while the point-precision designs advance.

3. Why would a binary file at ~0.7 % of the footprint score this well? (DERIVED + judgement)

From the exact rule (see utility-derivation.md), the score is DTI = TP / (α·S + β·G). Four consequences:

  1. Recall dominates. β = 0.8 against α = 0.2 means a missed fault costs four times a wrong dot. A sparse, confident set can still score if its hits are well placed.
  2. Break-even is about credit per dot, not density. At DTI ≈ 0.28 a dot pays only if its kernel credit exceeds ≈ 0.056, i.e. it sits within ≈ 283 m of a true fault. Dots beyond that are pure cost.
  3. Calibration is not the lever. Under the exact rule, the best per-pixel choice is a threshold on expected credit, which is why binary 0/1 output is natural. This is a heuristic argument, not a proof: the max over the kernel makes the objective non-linear. The 0.2778 file uses exactly that form.
  4. The mass budget is set by where the truth probably is. 0.73 % of the footprint is a deliberate, small set, placed where the model thinks faults are. It is not a random lattice.

So the most defensible account of why it scored is: a sparse binary set, chosen by a thresholded field, with recall weighted above precision by the official metric. That is judgement, not a measured attribution. We have one public score and no organizer decomposition.

4. What the 0.2778 file's own story gets wrong (IRREGULARITY, review)

  • The GEMSDOE32 README says the scorer "masks out" catalogue pixels and that dots on the

catalogue "cost α = 0.2 each and can earn nothing". Those two statements contradict each other.

  • DrivenData staff (2026-09-16) answered that pixels on known faults are "masked / excluded from

evaluation, so they do not count towards penalty terms." A dot on a known fault costs nothing.

  • So GEMSDOE32's pruning of catalogue dots ("0 dots within 200 m of the catalogue") does not change the

official score. Its own instrument (IR-32-INSTR-01, a 47 % discontinuity) appears to come from charging catalogue dots. That instrument is therefore miscalibrated against the official rule.

  • Consequence for us: any proxy that penalises known-fault dots will mis-rank candidates. Our holdout

must apply the same mask the organizers use.

utility-derivation

Utility derivation — when does adding a dot raise the score?

Status: algebra verified (500/500 random trials, scripts/utility_table.py, evidence/utility_table_synthetic.json). Labels: all numbers here are SYNTHETIC or PROJECTION. None is a holdout or organizer score.

1. Exact identity

Let G be the number of ground-truth pixels (after the known-fault mask), TP, FP, FN the distance-weighted quantities from the official problem page, and S = TP + FP.

  • TP + FN = G exactly, because TP = Σ_g M(g) and FN = Σ_g (1 − M(g)), where M(g) is the best kernel-weighted prediction within 300 m of truth pixel g.
  • Therefore the denominator TP + α·FP + β·FN = α·S + β·G (using α + β = 1).

So DTI = TP / (α·S + β·G), with α = 0.2, β = 0.8 (official).

2. Marginal rule (exact)

Write D = DTI. Adding mass changes TP by ΔT and S by ΔS. Then

sign(ΔD) = sign(ΔT − α·D·ΔS).

Proof: ΔD = [ΔT·(αS+βG) − T·α·ΔS] / (αS+βG)² and T = D·(αS+βG), so the numerator is (αS+βG)·(ΔT − αD·ΔS).

Consequence: a dot pays iff its gain in TP exceeds α·D times its gain in (TP+FP).

3. The single-pixel special case

For an isolated dot whose only effect is on one truth pixel at kernel credit k = 1 − d/R: ΔT = k, ΔFP = 1 − k, so ΔS = 1, and the dot pays iff k > α·D.

4. Why this is not "a density of 1–2 %"

The 1–2 % figure in the brief is a density heuristic. The rule above is about credit per dot, which depends on distance to truth and on overlap. Two consequences:

  • A dot can pay even at low density if it sits within ~280 m of a true fault.
  • A dense lattice can lose if most of its dots lie further than ~280 m from truth, because each one adds ΔS ≈ 1 and almost no ΔT.

Illustration only (PROJECTION, not measured): if a pixel lies within 300 m of an unmapped fault with probability π, and the average kernel credit given that is c, then expected credit is π·c. With c = 0.5, the break-even is π ≈ 0.11 at D = 0.28. The value of c is unknown until a holdout is run.

A holdout with real off-fault background is required, and until one exists the spacing rule is unconfirmed.

6. What is still needed

  1. A holdout on which TP, FP and FN can be measured for real emission sets (blocked until the data is placed).
  2. The estimated credit distribution E[k | x] for each candidate pixel, which the model must supply.
  3. Only then, choose emission by thresholding E[k | x] at α·D, where D is the holdout DTI of the chosen set (fixed point).

Candidates

H-1 (rank 1) — Thermal-spring alignment prior from an independent point inventory

  • Layers: none of the 19 official bands. New input: hot-spring and warm-spring locations with temperature or chemistry.
  • Physical signature: springs rise along permeable conduits, and fault zones are the main ones. Clusters and alignments of springs should follow structures that are unmapped in the catalogue, especially under alluvium where there is no surface trace.
  • Why it catches a fault the catalogue lacks: the spring record is independent of the USGS fault database, so it can locate a structure the catalogue has missed. This is the one candidate here whose information source is not the fault catalogue.
  • How it differs from the registry: none of the registry ideas uses spring locations. The closest is the drainage-valley chain (H33-B), which is also a valley-bottom signal. That is exactly the mimic, so it must be controlled.
  • Named non-fault mimic: valley-floor groundwater discharge, playa margins and drainage lines produce spring alignments with no fault beneath them. Control by regressing on distance to drainage and on basin depth (band 15).
  • Evidence for the physics (verified): a USGS publication reports that high-temperature systems follow young NE-trending faults and that extensional systems track strain rates. Source: <https://www.usgs.gov/publications/geothermal-gis-coverage-great-basin-usa-defining-regional-controls-and-favorable>. The same page documents a weights-of-evidence analysis.
  • Data obtainability: a USGS Science Data Catalog entry lists a 1974 hot-spring chemistry compilation (Mariner et al., OFR 74-1066) and a regional geophysical compilation. Listing verified: <https://data.usgs.gov/datacatalog/search?otherKeyword=%5B%22geothermal+resources%22%5D>. Download not verified from this sandbox; the next step is a manual download of the official file and a sha256 record.
  • Implementation cost: medium (point cleaning, deduplication, a distance-to-spring surface and a controlled regression).
  • Expected DTI gain: unknown; could be high if springs mark unmapped faults, nil if they mostly mark valleys. PROJECTION: unmeasured.
  • Validation plan: holdout on withheld segments only, with springs recomputed from visible data alone. The leakage canary applies: AUC above 0.90 on the spring feature alone means leakage until proven otherwise.

H-2 (rank 2, demoted) — Gravity horizontal-gradient maxima (Blakely & Simpson 1986), with a magnetic coincidence rule

  • Layers: isostatic gravity anomaly (band 13); total magnetic intensity (band 14) for coincidence.
  • Physical signature: density contrasts at basin-bounding faults show up as ridges of the horizontal-gradient magnitude, and the ridge loci are the body edges. Blakely & Simpson (1986) formalised locating those maxima: Geophysics 51:1494–1498, DOI 10.1190/1.1442197. Citation chain verified through Mayhew (1987) and a Sciencedirect paper on the method.
  • Why it could catch a missing fault: basin edges under alluvium often have no surface trace.
  • Overlap: high with H33-A (multi-physics oriented-lineament consensus), which already combines physics. This is a near-duplicate of a registry lane. Running it separately risks the drift rule (rho above 0.90 or more than 70 % dot overlap). Treat as a component of H33-A, not a new lane.
  • Expected DTI gain: low-to-medium, since it probably reproduces H33-A.
  • Cost: low (a few hundred lines on a grid already provided).

H-3 (rank 3) — A regional gravity compilation as an upgrade to the supplied gravity layers

  • Layers: the supplied isostatic gravity (bands 5, 11, 13, 18) is the baseline. The candidate is an independent regional gravity grid for the Great Basin, which the USGS Science Data Catalog lists as "regional geophysical maps of the Great Basin". Listing verified: <https://data.usgs.gov/datacatalog/search?otherKeyword=%5B%22geothermal+resources%22%5D>.
  • Physical signature: sharper density boundaries from better long-wavelength gravity, which feed the same edge tests as H-2.
  • Why it could catch a missing fault: the same as H-2, but with a different and possibly better gravity source. It is data-driven, so it is less likely to just reproduce a registry transform.
  • Obtainability: not verified (listing only). Datum and regridding to EPSG:32611 have not been checked.
  • Expected DTI gain: unknown; probably small unless the new grid is clearly better over the study area.
  • Cost: medium to high (datum, resampling, edge tests).
